AI Centres of Excellence in Russell Group universities

A benchmark of six universities, the themes they share, and recommendations for the University of Warwick · November 2026
Summary. Six universities were reviewed: Oxford, Cambridge, Imperial, UCL, Edinburgh and Manchester. Each has built some central function to drive AI adoption across the institution, but they use different names (competency centre, hub, task force, programme). Most of them share the same components:
  • a secure AI tool that the university provides
  • guidance on which tools to use and what data is allowed
  • training in levels, from beginner to advanced
  • a community of practice
  • visible governance, i.e. it is public who runs it and who is accountable
The most advanced add two more: seed funding for experiments, and a route for staff to build their own AI tools.

Warwick's AI Centre of Excellence sits within IDG, Warwick's central IT department. Its aims are strong. From the outside, its weak points are that its pages are hard to find, its governance isn't visible, and it shows no evidence of what it has delivered.
Method and limits. The evidence comes from web search results and page extracts gathered on 13 November 2026. University websites could not be opened directly from the research environment. Page lists may be incomplete, and some pages require a university login. Warwick's own AI Centre of Excellence page was not found, so the Warwick assessment is based on what is publicly visible: committee minutes, IDG pages and the library guide. This report compares what each university publishes. It does not measure how well their programmes work.

1. What each university has

University of Oxford: AI Competency Centre

Owner: Oxford e-Research Centre (OeRC) · Head: Alwyn Collinson, supported by consultants, research software engineers and trainers · Running since late 2024
  • Hub pages on ox.ac.uk: Generative AI at Oxford; About the Centre; Tools and policy; Training; AI Ambassadors and peer networks; Case studies; Projects in higher education.
  • The centre's own site on the OeRC domain (oerc.ox.ac.uk/ai-centre): About; Training; Workshops and webinars; AI guides (for example, GenAI for beginners and Getting started for educators); a ChatGPT Edu service page; News.
  • Tools: ChatGPT Edu for all staff and students since September 2025, plus Microsoft 365 Copilot and Copilot Chat.
  • Community: an AI Ambassadors network of more than 250 staff, Teams communities, and a presence at Freshers' Fair.
  • Staff intranet: a single sign-on page for the AI and ML Competency Centre.
ox.ac.uk/about/digital/gen-ai · oerc.ox.ac.uk/ai-centre

University of Cambridge: ai@cam, plus central services

Owners: ai@cam (the university's flagship AI mission), University Information Services (UIS, the central IT department), the Blended Learning Service and Information Compliance
  • ai@cam "AI in the University": two funded programmes.
    • AI for University Operations, for professional services teams.
    • AI for Teaching and Learning: six-month projects with up to £10k each.
    Both include an AI Clinic with machine learning engineers, roundtables where funded teams learn from each other, a community of practice, and a closing showcase.
  • Guidance:
    • Information Compliance has published staff guidance on using generative AI for administration. It is dated and on a review cycle.
    • The Blended Learning Service has published "Using Generative AI" for staff and students.
    • Every degree course (Tripos) must publish its own AI guidance for students.
  • Training: a three-module GenAI Literacy Course on Moodle, and a UIS "Google AI Academy" (a kick-off session, then a workshop, then office hours).
  • Tools: Microsoft Copilot Chat, Gemini and NotebookLM. The free public versions are banned for university work.
ai.cam.ac.uk/ai-in-the-university · cam.ac.uk/ai · information-compliance.admin.cam.ac.uk/…/ai-guidance · blendedlearning.cam.ac.uk/artificial-intelligence-and-education

Imperial College London: ICT AI hub, the dAIsy platform and an AI strategy

Owner: ICT (Imperial's IT department), with Education and the Library · The AI strategy and training programme was announced in July 2026
  • AI front door: a task-based tool finder. Staff pick what they want to do (write, ask questions of Imperial documents, code, get API access, automate a workflow) and are pointed to the approved tool for it.
  • dAIsy: Imperial's own platform giving access to several models (GPT, Claude, DeepSeek). It has:
    • official agents, plus personal agents that staff build themselves
    • developer access by request
    • a use policy, and a page listing the available models
    • a matrix of what each user group may do
  • Other tools: Copilot, GitHub Copilot, the ASK chatbot for IT and service questions, and HEX AI.
  • Guidance and training: Generative AI Principles; separate GenAI hubs for education (staff) and study (students); Introduction to GenAI courses for staff and for students; Library referencing guidance.
  • Strategy: "AI for the Workplace" training in levels that build on each other; a new Centre for AI in Learning and Teaching; a Professional Services and AI Working Group; and the "AI Futurists" community. Imperial also states the risks it is managing: dependency on suppliers, cost, sustainability and supplier behaviour.
imperial.ac.uk/admin-services/ict/ai · …/ict/training-and-resources/daisy

UCL: AI Education Programme, Generative AI Hub and AI@UCL

Owners: Teaching and Learning (the Arena Centre for staff development) and ISD (UCL's IT department)
  • Generative AI Hub: a structured guide covering what GenAI is; how it works; academic integrity; prompting; capabilities and limits; ethics and sustainability; data safeguarding; and GenAI in work and study. It also includes a teaching toolkit and staff events.
  • AI in Education programme: seven workstreams, each with named leads:
    • strategy and governance
    • digital technologies
    • curriculum
    • assessment
    • student voice
    • staff development
    • pedagogic research
    It also offers up to £10k per project for research and scholarship on AI in teaching.
  • AI@UCL (pilot): a managed "AI Store" with two agents, UCL Essentials (powered by GPT) and UCL Insights (powered by Claude). An "AI Foundry", where staff can build their own tools, is planned.
  • Training: a GenAI staff training programme with recordings on SharePoint, the "GenAI Fundamentals" webinar series, and a GenAI and academic skills course for students.
ucl.ac.uk/teaching-learning/generative-ai-hub · ucl.ac.uk/teaching-learning/education-excellence/ai-education · ucl.ac.uk/isd/aiucl

University of Edinburgh: AI Adoption Task Force and Hub, plus the ELM platform

Owners: the AI Adoption Task Force (chair: Prof. Michael Rovatsos) and Information Services Group / EDINA (the university's digital services unit) · Hub launched October 2024
  • AI Adoption Hub: a SharePoint site plus a Teams community. Its remit covers protecting assessment integrity, keeping a catalogue of AI projects across the university, and AI governance.
  • ELM (Edinburgh access to Language Models): a platform the university built itself. It offers:
    • several models, with a zero-data-retention agreement with OpenAI
    • a self-hosted open-source model (Llama 3.3 70B), with a published energy cost per query
    • API keys so staff can build their own applications
  • ELM Competency Centre: an introduction to ELM, use-case examples, and specific guidance on agentic AI.
  • Guidance: AI principles; Responsible AI and AI safety; guidelines for students; guidance for staff (including when an AI project needs ethics board approval); and an AI Impact Assessment.
  • Training: a catalogue of courses with published completion figures, for example 1,080 staff completions of the introductory course since January 2025. Courses include prompt clinics, ELM for programming, and AI for job applications.
ed.ac.uk/ai/guidance · information-services.ed.ac.uk/computing/comms-and-collab/elm

University of Manchester: AI Hub (StaffNet) and AI Transformation Programme

Owners: the University AI Strategy Group (chair: Prof. Chris Taylor), a Professional Services AI Strategy Group, and an AI Upskilling Working Group · Microsoft partnership since January 2026
  • AI Hub: AI Guidelines and Policies; the Pathfinder Programme (staff submit AI ideas and get help turning them into projects); Approved Tools; Training and Support; a page on the strategy groups; and a single contact address.
  • Principles: Transparency, Accountability, Competence, Responsible Use and Respect. These sit alongside an AI in Teaching and Learning Policy for 2026.
  • Tools: Microsoft 365 Copilot for all 65,000 staff and students, the first university in the world to do this. A NebulaONE model catalogue is also offered.
  • Training: a 20-minute AI Essentials course; Copilot Champions; a Copilot Skills community; and a needs hub for AI in teaching and learning. It publishes results: more than 4,000 attendees, and staff confidence rising from 24% to 80%.
  • Student involvement: a Copilot Student Advisory Group, and Library guides on AI.
staffnet.manchester.ac.uk/ai-hub · itservices.manchester.ac.uk/ourservices/microsoft365/copilot

3. Underlying themes

Theme 1: The secure tool comes first and everything else is built around it

Every university's AI function is organised around one university-provided tool with data protection. The model is either buy or build.

  • Buy: Manchester (Microsoft 365 Copilot) and Oxford (ChatGPT Edu).
  • Build: Edinburgh (ELM), Imperial (dAIsy), and UCL (AI@UCL, still in pilot).

The trade-offs:

  • Buying is fast and scales well, but creates dependency on one supplier.
  • Building gives choice of model, data control and an API, but costs engineering time and needs ongoing maintenance.

Imperial is the only one to say the trade-off out loud: it names dependency, cost and supplier behaviour as risks it manages.

Theme 2: Guidance is organised by task and audience, not by policy document

The strongest sites route people by role (staff, students, researchers, professional services) or by task ("I want to summarise / code / automate"). Imperial's "what do you want to do?" page is the clearest example. Policy still exists, but it sits behind a practical decision aid rather than in front of it.

Theme 3: Training comes in levels, and the leaders measure the results

Everyone offers training from introductory to advanced. Only Edinburgh and Manchester publish numbers: completions, attendance, and change in staff confidence. That is the line between running training and running a change programme.

Theme 4: Adoption spreads through people, not web pages

Each university has a peer network:

  • Oxford: AI Ambassadors (250+)
  • Manchester: Copilot Champions
  • Edinburgh: a Teams community
  • Imperial: AI Futurists

Small central teams use these networks to reach a decentralised institution.

Theme 5: The more mature centres fund experiments, not just provide access

  • Cambridge: £10k, six-month projects, with AI Clinic support and a closing showcase.
  • UCL: £10k for research and scholarship on AI in teaching.
  • Manchester: Pathfinder.

These schemes produce evidence and case studies, which in turn justify further investment. Access alone does not.

Theme 6: Education and operations run on separate tracks

Teaching, assessment and academic integrity are owned academically:

  • UCL: Arena
  • Imperial: the new Centre for AI in Learning and Teaching
  • Cambridge: each degree course writes its own guidance

Tools, data and productivity are owned by IT or digital services. Where IT owns both, credibility with academics tends to suffer.

Theme 7: Governance is made visible

Manchester lists its strategy groups and their chairs, UCL names leads for seven workstreams, and Edinburgh names its task force chair. Publishing who is accountable builds trust and tells staff who to contact.

Theme 8: Everyone agrees on the same principles

The principles are broadly the same everywhere: transparency, accountability, verifying outputs, protecting data, ethics and sustainability. That means principles are not what sets a university apart. What sets it apart is execution.

4. Where Warwick stands

What's visible publicly:

  • The AI Centre of Excellence is hosted on IDG's web pages. Its stated aims, from the Learning and Teaching Subcommittee minutes of February 2025, are to:
    • foster innovation
    • ensure ethics and inclusivity
    • accelerate research
    • personalise education
    • optimise administration
  • Policy and strategy: an AI policy and AI strategy were in draft as of February 2025; an AI strategy PDF is now published on the IDG site; there is also an AI Information Compliance Policy.
  • Tools: Microsoft Copilot is the approved AI tool. Access to other AI tools and models, such as Claude, is requested through the Technology Approval Process.
  • Education: work is led by the Academic Development Centre, IATL and staff learning circles.
  • Signposting: the Library's AI guide is the best existing index of Warwick's AI resources.

Assessment. Warwick's stated aims compare well with its peers. The gap is in how visible the centre is and how well it is put into practice. In the public evidence, the centre:

  • has no front door that is easy to find
  • names no team and no accountable leads
  • has no funding scheme for experiments
  • publishes no metrics

Its home inside IT also puts it at risk of the problem described in Theme 6: academics may see it as an IT service rather than as an academic partner.

Caveat: some of these components may exist behind single sign-on. If so, the first recommendation still stands, because a centre that staff cannot find does not function as a centre of excellence.

5. Recommendations for Warwick

The recommendations are ordered by impact relative to effort. Each one names the peer model it draws on.

1Build one public front door, routed by task and audience. Give the centre one memorable URL (for example, warwick.ac.uk/ai). It should route people to sections for staff, students, research and professional services, and include a "what do you want to do?" tool finder. Model: Imperial ICT AI and Oxford Gen AI. Low cost, and it fixes the biggest gap.
2Publish the governance and the team. Name the head of the centre and the strategy or steering groups, and state the current status of the AI policy and strategy. Model: Manchester's strategy groups and UCL's named workstream leads. This costs almost nothing and buys accountability.
3Make Copilot work well, and make the route to other tools visible. With Copilot as the approved tool, the value comes from clarity rather than more tools:
  • Publish a plain "what data can I use in Copilot?" guide.
  • Give the Technology Approval Process its own page on the hub. Explain how to request access to other AI tools and models, such as Claude, with the criteria, the expected turnaround time and a contact.
  • Record each approval decision in the Use Case Register so other departments can reuse it instead of applying again.
  • Set a review date for the tool decision.
Model: Cambridge, which recommends its licensed tools and sends other tools through procurement and risk assessment. Without a visible route, staff use unapproved tools anyway, which creates data risk.
4Launch a small seed-fund with an AI clinic. Offer around £5–10k for six-month projects, with technical support and a closing showcase, on two tracks: operations and teaching. Model: Cambridge ai@cam. This is the cheapest way to generate Warwick-specific evidence and case studies.
5Set up a champions network. Recruit volunteer AI champions in each department, supported by the centre. Model: Oxford Ambassadors (250+) and Manchester Copilot Champions. Build on the existing staff learning circles rather than starting from scratch.
6Run tiered training and publish the numbers. Make a short essentials course mandatory and offer role-based modules above it. Report completions and confidence before and after. Model: Manchester (24% to 80% confidence) and Edinburgh's published completion counts. Measure what people actually use AI for as well as how many attend; attendance alone is a vanity metric.
7Formally split the education track from the IT track. Let the Academic Development Centre and IATL own pedagogy, assessment and integrity, and let IDG own tools, data and productivity. Both should be visible from the single front door. Model: UCL Arena with ISD, and Imperial's Centre for AI in Learning and Teaching with ICT.
8Publish a case-study library and an AI project register. Record what has been tried, what worked and what was stopped. Model: Oxford case studies and Edinburgh's project catalogue. This prevents duplicated effort and gives the centre a record of what it has delivered.
Risks to manage
  • Access is not value. Universal licences, as at Manchester, make strong headlines, but the evidence on productivity gains is still thin. Define the outcomes up front (time saved, service quality, student results) instead of counting seats.
  • Copying the form instead of the function. Page structure is easy to copy. Named owners, funding and measurement are what make these centres work.
  • Vendor lock-in. Whichever tool route Warwick chooses, keep data portable and review contracts on a fixed schedule.
  • Academic legitimacy. A centre led by IT that issues teaching guidance will meet resistance. Co-ownership with academics is not optional.
